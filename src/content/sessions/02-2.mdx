<Section kicker="Session 2">The point</Section>

<p>Two vowels meeting is awkward to say. Italian avoids it constantly, and this is the first place it
shows up: before a noun starting with a vowel, both <em>il</em> and <em>la</em> shorten to
<em>l'</em>.</p>

<Rule lead="Before a vowel → l', whatever the gender">
<WordTable words={[{"term": "l'armadio", "say": "lar-MA-dyo", "gloss": "the wardrobe — masculine"}, {"term": "l'aula", "say": "LOW-la", "gloss": "the classroom — feminine"}, {"term": "l'astuccio", "say": "las-TOO-cho", "gloss": "the pencil case — masculine"}, {"term": "l'orario", "say": "lo-RA-ryo", "gloss": "the timetable — masculine"}, {"term": "l'ora", "say": "LO-ra", "gloss": "the hour — feminine"}]} />
  <Lead spaced>Say it as one word</Lead>
  <p>The apostrophe is not a pause. <em>L'aula</em> is two syllables and sounds like a single word —
  which is the whole reason the language does it. If they are saying "luh — aula", they have not got
  it yet.</p>
</Rule>

<Warn lead="This is where gender gets lost">
<p><em>L'armadio</em> is masculine and <em>l'aula</em> is feminine, and the article is identical.
So for these nouns the article is no longer doing the remembering for you, and the gender has to be
learned with the word itself. It matters immediately in Unit 3, where the plurals go different ways
— and by then the article will not be there to remind them.</p>
</Warn>

<h3>How to teach it</h3>

<Steps>
<li>Add the line to the article table in the notebook. It is one line and it goes under the two from last session.</li>
  <li>Say these five aloud, as single words, until the join disappears: <Grid words={["l'armadio", "l'aula", "l'astuccio", "l'orario", "l'ora"]} /></li>
  <li>Then the test that matters: say the noun on its own and ask for the gender. <em>Armadio?</em> Masculine. <em>Aula?</em> Feminine. If that is slow, the word was learned as a sound and not as a gendered noun, and it needs going back over.</li>
</Steps>

<Note lead="Back to Unit 1 for a moment">
<p>This is the same instinct as the silent <em>i</em> in <em>ciao</em> — Italian bending spelling to
keep the mouth moving smoothly. Point that out. A rule that connects to something they already own
is one rule; a rule on its own is another thing to carry.</p>
</Note>
