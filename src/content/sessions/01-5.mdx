<p>Teach these as sound first, meaning second. Every one of them exercises something from Sessions 1–4:
<em>ciao</em> (silent i), <em>buongiorno</em> (soft g, gn), <em>chiamo</em> (hard ch), <em>grazie</em>
(the ts sound), <em>arrivederci</em> (soft ci, double r). Point that out as you go.</p>

<Notebook lead={"In the notebook"}>
<p>Copy the fifteen greetings with their meanings. This is the first Italian vocabulary they write
  down all course, so set the habit here: Italian on the left, English on the right, and the
  pronunciation only where it surprises them.</p>
  <p>Then write the exchange out in full, both parts, as a dialogue with a dash for each speaker — the
  way it is set out below. Writing it before saying it makes the recording steadier, and it is the first
  Italian conversation they have ever put on paper.</p>
  <p><strong>Do this before the recording, not after.</strong> The sixteen-word list in Part 1 is never
  written down — reading it unseen is the entire point of it.</p>
</Notebook>

<Section kicker={"Then"}>The baseline recording</Section>

<p>Record each child separately. Two parts, three or four minutes in total.</p>

<h3>Part 1 — reading unfamiliar words</h3>

<p>Give them this list cold, with no preparation, and record them reading it aloud. These sixteen words
are deliberately not in any flashcard deck — they are the diagnostic, and you will use the same list
again in six months.</p>

<h3>Part 2 — the greetings exchange</h3>

<p>You take one part, they take the other:</p>

<Note lead={"Save both files"}>
<p>Wherever you keep recordings, named with the child and the date, and logged in the progress
  tracker so you can find them again. Do not correct or re-record for a better take. The value of this recording is entirely in it being honest. In six months you will play it back
  and the difference will be obvious to them, which is worth more than any mark.</p>
</Note>
