<Section kicker="Session 4">The point</Section>

<p>Everything decided in the last three sessions decides this one too. There is no new thinking —
only a second set of words for the decisions already made.</p>

<Rule lead="The same three questions, a different answer each time">
<WordTable words={[{"term": "il → un", "say": "oon", "gloss": "<em>il libro</em> → <em>un libro</em>"}, {"term": "lo → uno", "say": "OO-no", "gloss": "<em>lo studente</em> → <em>uno studente</em>"}, {"term": "la → una", "say": "OO-na", "gloss": "<em>la sedia</em> → <em>una sedia</em>"}, {"term": "l' → un or un'", "say": "", "gloss": "and this is the one that catches people"}]} />
</Rule>

<Warn lead="The trap: un amico, but un'amica">
<p>Before a vowel the definite article was <em>l'</em> for both genders, so nothing had to be
decided. The indefinite article does not let you off: masculine takes <strong>un</strong> with no
apostrophe, feminine takes <strong>un'</strong> with one.</p>
<p><em>un amico</em> — masculine, no apostrophe. <em>un'amica</em> — feminine, apostrophe.</p>
<p>So the gender they were told to keep hold of in Session 2, when <em>l'</em> stopped showing it,
is needed here. That is why that session insisted on it.</p>
</Warn>

<h3>How to teach it</h3>

<Steps>
<li>Build the full table in the notebook. Four rows, two columns, and it is the reference for the next two years — this is the page they will come back to.</li>
  <li>Take the words from all three sessions and go through them twice: once saying <em>the</em>, once saying <em>a</em>. <Grid words={["libro", "sedia", "studente", "aula", "zio", "armadio", "penna", "spazzolino", "amico", "amica"]} /></li>
  <li>Then the apostrophe test on its own: <em>un amico</em> and <em>un'amica</em>, written down, side by side, and the difference said out loud. It is one mark on paper and it is worth a minute of its own.</li>
</Steps>

<Notebook lead="In the notebook">
<p>The finished table, on the fifth page, under everything else:</p>
  <WriteOut>
<strong>il</strong> — <strong>un</strong>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; ordinary masculine<br />
    <strong>lo</strong> — <strong>uno</strong>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; masculine, s+consonant / z / gn / ps / y<br />
    <strong>la</strong> — <strong>una</strong>&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; ordinary feminine<br />
    <strong>l'</strong> — <strong>un</strong> (m) / <strong>un'</strong> (f)&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; before a vowel
</WriteOut>
</Notebook>

<Section kicker="Then">The labels</Section>

<p>This is the task the unit has been building towards, and it is worth doing properly.</p>

<Steps>
<li>Write a label for every noun in this unit that is a thing you can find — a sticky note, or paper and tape. The article goes on the label. <strong>Never the bare noun.</strong></li>
  <li>They write them, not you. Handwriting the pair is half the point, and it is examined in two of the four papers.</li>
  <li>Stick each one on the thing itself. <em>la sedia</em> on a chair, <em>l'armadio</em> on the wardrobe, <em>lo spazzolino</em> on a toothbrush, <em>la finestra</em> on a window.</li>
  <li><strong>Leave them up for a month.</strong> Not a week. The value is in walking past <em>lo spazzolino</em> every morning without trying to learn it.</li>
</Steps>

<Note lead="Read them aloud when you pass one" child>
<p>The labels do nothing if they are only ever seen. For the first week, say the pair aloud whenever
you happen to be at one — no lesson, no sitting down, three seconds. That is what turns a label into
a word they own.</p>
</Note>
