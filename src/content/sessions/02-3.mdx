<Section kicker="Session 3">The point</Section>

<p>Start with the mouth, not the rule. Have them say this aloud, quickly, three times:</p>

<p><em>il studente, il studente, il studente</em></p>

<p>It is genuinely uncomfortable — <em>l</em> then <em>st</em> is a pile-up of consonants. Now
<em>lo studente</em>. The vowel gives the mouth somewhere to go. <strong>That is the entire
reason <em>lo</em> exists</strong>, and a child who has felt it does not need to memorise it.</p>

<Rule lead="Before a consonant cluster the mouth cannot easily start → lo">
<WordTable words={[{"term": "s + another consonant", "say": "", "gloss": "<em>lo studente</em>, <em>lo stadio</em>, <em>lo spazzolino</em>"}, {"term": "z", "say": "", "gloss": "<em>lo zucchero</em>, <em>lo zio</em> — z is really <em>ts</em>, two sounds"}, {"term": "gn, ps, y", "say": "", "gloss": "rarer, same reason"}]} />
  <Lead spaced>Everything else stays il</Lead>
  <p><em>Il sole</em>, not <em>lo sole</em> — <em>s</em> followed by a vowel is easy to say, so
  nothing changes. It is the <strong>cluster</strong> that triggers it, never the letter
  <em>s</em> on its own.</p>
</Rule>

<Note lead="Why z counts">
<p><em>Z</em> looks like one letter and is two sounds — <em>ts</em>. So <em>lo zucchero</em> is
really <em>lo tsucchero</em>, and it is a consonant cluster like all the others. This is the same
observation as Unit 1, where a single written letter turned out to be two sounds. The spelling hides
the sound; the sound makes the rule.</p>
</Note>

<h3>How to teach it</h3>

<Steps>
<li>Do the <em>il studente</em> experiment above before anything is written down. The feeling comes first or the rule is just another line.</li>
  <li>Add it to the article table. One line, and it earns the arrow: <strong>il → lo</strong> before a cluster.</li>
  <li>Sort these ten out loud, <em>il</em> or <em>lo</em>, saying the whole pair each time: <Grid words={["studente", "zucchero", "libro", "spazzolino", "tavolo", "zio", "sport", "quaderno", "stadio", "schermo"]} /></li>
  <li>Then the one that proves it: ask for <em>il sole</em> — the sun. It starts with s. Most learners say <em>lo sole</em>. Let them, then have them say both aloud and pick the comfortable one.</li>
</Steps>

<Answers lead="Answers">
<p><strong>lo</strong> — studente, zucchero, spazzolino, zio, sport, stadio, schermo.<br />
  <strong>il</strong> — libro, tavolo, quaderno.<br />
  And <em>il sole</em>, because <em>so-</em> is a consonant and a vowel, not a cluster.</p>
</Answers>

<Note lead="Do not accept a right answer without a reason" child>
<p>A child can get all ten right by copying the pattern from the last one. Ask "why?" on at least
three of them, and accept only an answer about the sound. "Because it starts with st" is the
answer; "because it looks like the other one" is not, and it will fall apart in Unit 6 when the
same instinct governs verb spellings.</p>
</Note>

<DoneWhen lead="Done when">
<p>They can state the rule in their own words, sort the ten without help, and say why
<em>il sole</em> keeps its <em>il</em>. This is the can-do the whole unit turns on — the same way
the hard and soft rule was Unit 1's.</p>
</DoneWhen>
