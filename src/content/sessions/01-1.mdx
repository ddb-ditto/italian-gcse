<Section kicker={"Session 1"}>The point</Section>

<p>Italian has five vowel sounds. Five. They are always the same, they are never swallowed, and they
never turn into the lazy "uh" that English does at the end of words. This is the single biggest thing
that makes a learner sound Italian rather than English-speaking-Italian.</p>

<Rule lead={"The five, and what they are not"}>
<WordTable words={[{"term": "a", "say": "ah", "gloss": "as in <em>father</em> — never as in <em>cat</em>"}, {"term": "e", "say": "eh", "gloss": "as in <em>bet</em>, or the vowel in <em>day</em> without the y-glide at the end"}, {"term": "i", "say": "ee", "gloss": "as in <em>machine</em> — never as in <em>bit</em>"}, {"term": "o", "say": "oh", "gloss": "as in <em>pot</em> or <em>for</em> — never the English \"oh\" with a w on the end"}, {"term": "u", "say": "oo", "gloss": "as in <em>boot</em> — never as in <em>but</em>, never the English \"you\" sound"}]} />
</Rule>

<h3>How to teach it</h3>
<Steps>
<li>Say each vowel five times slowly. Have them copy. Exaggerate.</li>
  <li>The critical instruction: <strong>the mouth doesn't move while the sound is happening.</strong> English vowels slide — say "no" slowly and you will hear it become "now". Italian vowels are held in one position. Get them to hold a hand under the jaw to feel whether it is moving.</li>
  <li>Drill these words, which contain nothing but clean vowels. Every letter is pronounced; there are no silent letters in Italian. <Grid words={["casa", "vino", "luna", "sole", "mare", "pane", "nome", "vita"]} /></li>
  <li>Say each word wrong (English-style) and right, and ask them which is which. Making them the judge is more effective than making them the imitator.</li>
</Steps>

<Notebook lead={"In the notebook"}>
<p>Date the page, then rule three columns and copy this out. It becomes the reference page they come
  back to for the next two years, so it is worth doing neatly.</p>
  <WordTable words={[{"term": "a", "say": "like father", "gloss": "never like cat"}, {"term": "e", "say": "like bet", "gloss": "never like day"}, {"term": "i", "say": "like machine", "gloss": "never like bit"}, {"term": "o", "say": "like pot", "gloss": "never like oh"}, {"term": "u", "say": "like boot", "gloss": "never like but"}]} />
  <p>Underneath, copy the eight words — <em>casa, vino, luna, sole, mare, pane, nome, vita</em> — saying
  each one aloud as they write it, not before or after. Then <strong>underline the last letter of every
  word</strong>. That underline is the whole point: the final vowel is the one English speakers swallow,
  and they will see it on the page every time they look back.</p>
</Notebook>

<Note lead={"Common problem"}>
<p>The final vowel gets swallowed — <em>casa</em> becomes "cas-uh". Insist the final vowel is as
  strong as the first. Overcorrect at this stage; it will settle.</p>
</Note>

<Note lead={"If a child is working on clarity of speech"} child>
<p>This session plays directly into that. Holding a vowel steady and finishing a word properly is the
  same physical skill drama and speech exams ask for. Worth saying so out loud — it makes the drill feel
  less arbitrary.</p>
</Note>

<DoneWhen lead={"Done when"}>
<p>They can say all five vowels without the sound sliding, and finish <em>casa</em>, <em>mare</em> and
  <em>pane</em> with the final vowel as strong as the first.</p>
</DoneWhen>
